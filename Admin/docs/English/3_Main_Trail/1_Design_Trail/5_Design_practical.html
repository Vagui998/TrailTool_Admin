The term learning environment refers to the context in which different partners collaborate. This also translates into the physical locations where module participants learn and work. The types of learning environments below help clarify this. This overview presents a continuum — a seamless line with the educational context on one end and the work/societal context on the other. Within this continuum, the following learning environments can be distinguished (Bouw, 2020; Bos et al., 2024): 

  1. Learning environments based on alignment 

 Learners move back and forth between the separate contexts of education and external environments. 

 Targeted (group) interventions such as preparation sessions, return days, and coaching conversations help align education with the external context. 

  1. Learning environments based on incorporation 

 Part of the educational context is incorporated into the external context, or vice versa. 

 For example, aspects can be practiced in a simulated setting at the educational location. 

 In the external context, learners might receive just-in-time theoretical instruction or guided practice with skills. 

  1. Learning environments based on hybridization 

Parts of the educational and external contexts merge to create a new practice, with characteristics of both. 


Physical Location 

Conducting transdisciplinary education within the educational context has the advantage of well-equipped spaces for work, learning, and collaboration — think projectors, screens, tables, chairs, and Wi-Fi. Moreover, the campus is accessible and familiar to students, which can lower the threshold for participation. For societal partners, however, the campus may lie outside their comfort zone. 

Choosing an external location can be inspiring and may expose existing complex challenges that participants can address. This setting offers space for experimentation, research, and collaboration — often with societal partners already involved at the location. It also breaks the routine of a familiar environment, creating a neutral space where all participants engage on equal footing (Wagemakers et al., 2024). 

It’s crucial to consider the context: 

What kind of environment do you want to create with the participants?  
Should it be informal, practical, or social? 
The choice depends on the complex issue at hand. 

At Avans University of Applied Sciences, for example, HELIOs (Hybrid External Learning and Innovation Environments) go beyond the classroom. 

  • The Kleine Aarde food garden focuses on issues like food transition, biodiversity, and soil. 

  • The WijWest community center addresses social challenges such as neighborhood loneliness or youth crime.
