It’s important to identify and surface diversity — you can ask questions about differences. This approach is based on the Co-Design Canvas by Wina Smeenk (2023). 

Diversity in Interest 

As a team member, you participate in a transdisciplinary project for different reasons — personal, financial, or work-related. Ask yourself: Who has which interests, and why? 

 Be honest. These interests are not always visible. If they remain unspoken, the process can become complex and frustrating. So speak up, help resolve conflicts constructively, and contribute to better and more creative solutions. 

Diversity in Power 

Transdisciplinary collaboration almost always involves unequal power dynamics. A person’s role, network, knowledge, or personality can influence their power. If these dynamics are ignored or left unspoken, they can lead to frustration. 

 But power can also be used constructively (Het Groene Brein, 2023). Dreaming together about the future and your shared goal is a first step. Talk about how achieving the shared goal will shift the positions of all parties — you’re creating a new playing field. This means powerful players may need to give up or change things. The shared goal takes precedence over individual interests. 

Diversity in Knowledge 

The variety of knowledge types among team members is the foundation of your project. This includes a mix of scientific and practical insights that contribute to: 

  • System knowledge (understanding current reality) 

  • Goal knowledge (visualizing the desired outcome) 

  • Transformation knowledge (moving from system knowledge to goal knowledge) 

 (Het Groene Brein, 2023) 

Participants bring different types of knowledge from their experiences or lifestyles. Make visible what each person’s knowledge, expertise, and experience are — and what kind of knowledge they can and want to contribute.
