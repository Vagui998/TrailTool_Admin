Different practices: who brings in the substantive expertise? 

When designing transdisciplinary education, it’s important to consider who contributes the disciplinary perspectives — the students or the teachers. This directly affects your role as an educator. 

  1. Student-intensive education 
    In general interdisciplinary projects, students bring their own disciplinary knowledge. You guide the learning process as a guide on the side and don’t need to possess all the substantive expertise yourself. However, this requires a safe learning environment where students actively share their knowledge and learn from each other. 

  1. Teacher-intensive education 

 In narrow interdisciplinarity, teachers provide the various perspectives. You then take on a more traditional role (sage on the stage) and offer content frameworks or knowledge transfer. Students are relatively more passive in this setup. 

Both forms can be effective but require different design choices and teaching skills. Often, a student-intensive approach is preferred because it aligns better with contemporary, active learning and is less vulnerable to teacher absence. 

Teaching formats and organization: team teaching or not? 

To ensure all relevant perspectives are included in the education, co-teaching with a multidisciplinary team of educators is a logical choice. However, this is costly, vulnerable, and organizationally complex. Therefore, a common approach is to have one main facilitator (coach), with additional content contributions from others via guest lectures, online resources, or external experts.
